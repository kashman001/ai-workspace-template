Where AI Actually Helps

Everyone is adding AI to how they build software. The best research says that alone makes delivery worse. This is a map of where AI genuinely earns its place — with the evidence behind every claim.

The paradox

More AI, worse delivery?

−1.5%

delivery throughput, per 25% increase in AI adoption

−7.2%

delivery stability, per the same increase

DORA 2024 — the largest annual study of software delivery (survey-based, correlational; program home: dora.dev). Its 2025 follow-up, with 90% of ~5,000 respondents using AI, lands on the explanation: AI is an amplifier of existing organizational strengths and dysfunctions.

So where does AI actually earn its place — and what has to be true for it to pay off? That's this map.

If you take away three things

The whole map in three claims

  1. AI can lead where a machine can check the output — code, tests, contracts, triage. Where the artifact carries judgment or authority — priorities, go/no-go, sign-off — AI drafts and a human decides.

  2. Only two techniques have published production results, and both are ways of auto-writing tests behind objective pass/fail filters. Every other AI claim rests on practice patterns or judgment.

  3. AI amplifies what you already are. It raises change volume and speed, so your human checkpoints carry more load — fix the system before you accelerate it.

The rubric

Not all “AI helps” is equal

Every AI claim on the map carries one of four evidence tiers — and each tier implies an adoption stance.

TierMeansStance
[measured]Published deployment results from a named engineering orgAdopt where the pattern matches
[established]Widely practiced pattern; tooling claims varyPilot before standardizing
[heuristic]Usable, with known failure modesPilot with a human override
[hype]Vendor-claimed; no independent evidenceAvoid until evidence appears

Scope honesty: the testing and QA tiers come from published research; product and operations tiers apply the same rubric by author judgment.

The dividing line

One rule decides who leads

If a machine can check the output, AI can lead. If the artifact carries judgment or authority, AI drafts — a human decides.

AI can lead output a machine can check

  • Code with tests, gated by objective pass/fail filters
  • API contracts & breaking-change checks
  • Test-coverage diffs against the acceptance criteria
  • Bug triage & ready-to-work briefs
  • Release notes, changelogs, doc regeneration
  • Decision records — captured as a by-product of the work

Humans hold the pen artifacts that encode judgment or authority

  • Priorities & roadmaps
  • Go / no-go and release sign-off
  • User-acceptance verdicts
  • Rollback criteria — when to pull a release back
  • Reliability targets — how much failure is acceptable
  • Gate policy — which checks can block a change from shipping

The strongest evidence

The only measured wins, with numbers

Gated test generation [measured]

Every AI-written test must build, pass repeatedly, and measurably increase coverage — the share of code the tests actually exercise — before a human ever sees it.

Meta, FSE 2024 · 73% of surfaced tests accepted by engineers; 25% of generated tests raised coverage

AI-boosted fuzzing [measured]

Fuzzing bombards code with generated inputs to shake out crashes and security holes. LLM-written fuzz targets reached code human effort never covered.

Google OSS-Fuzz · ~30% coverage gains across 300+ projects; 26 new vulnerabilities by late 2024, incl. a 20-year-old OpenSSL flaw

The tag travels with the technique, not the stage. Reuse the gated pattern elsewhere — a regression test with every bug fix — and the evidence comes with it. Everything else on the map is [established] or below.

Where the pitch outruns the proof

Two pitches to resist

Self-healing tests [hype]

UI tests that auto-repair themselves when the app changes. The risk: silently accepting the very behavior changes your tests existed to catch. No independent evidence of efficacy.

Autonomous test agents [hype]

“Point it at your app; it designs and runs the tests.” Full autonomy over what gets tested — with no published deployment evidence behind it.

The filter is the rubric: these are the two loudest vendor pitches in the space — ask for independent, published deployment evidence. Both categories have none, which is exactly what the [hype] tag means.

Resolving the paradox

Why more AI can mean worse delivery

  1. AI trades writing time for review time. Generated changes still need human verification — and on some tasks the trade is net-negative.

  2. The bottleneck moves to the humans. As change volume rises, the constraint becomes review capacity and the test pipeline — not how fast code gets written.

Neither is an argument for less AI. They're an argument about where the next investment goes.

Resolving the paradox

Invest in the seams, not just the stages

The seams between stages carry the extra load — they're where AI-driven volume lands first:

Intake

How user signal becomes requirements, instead of evaporating.

Gates

Which checks can block a change from shipping — and who decides.

Review

The human checkpoint AI volume hits first; its latency sets your pace.

Feedback routing

Production learnings finding their way back to discovery.

One internal audit bears this out: checking our own AI-agent-driven workspace against this map, both high-severity gaps were seams — feedback intake and gate policy — not stages.

What to do with this

The asks

Leadership — three decisions what this deck is asking you to approve

  • Make the evidence tier the approval bar for AI tooling — [hype] doesn't get budget
  • Fund one seam alongside codegen seats: review capacity and gate policy first
  • Approve a gated test-generation pilot; judge it on acceptance rate and coverage, the way Meta did

Engineers — three operating rules how to apply the map day to day

  • Adopt the measured patterns; pilot established ones before standardizing on them
  • Keep a human override on anything [heuristic]
  • Ask vendors for independent evidence before trusting “self-healing” or “autonomous” pitches
